7 Balance Sheet Red Flags to Check Before You Calculate a Single Ratio

Most ratio mistakes are not formula mistakes. They happen because the statements don't hang together and nobody checked before calculating. These seven checks take a few minutes and can save a wrong conclusion.

1. Total assets don't equal liabilities + equity

Even a small difference matters. It usually means a missing line (a provision, a lease liability, a minority interest) or a sign error. Every ratio for that period is built on numbers that are wrong somewhere.

Check: difference = total assets − (total liabilities + equity). It should be zero in every period, to the unit.

2. The gap equals net income

If the balance-sheet difference exactly equals the year's net income, the profit was never posted to equity. This is common when statements are typed in by hand or exported before closing. ROE and debt-to-equity for that year are then wrong.

3. Retained earnings don't roll forward

Closing retained earnings ≈ opening retained earnings + net income − dividends (± other adjustments). If the movement doesn't reconcile, something is missing: a dividend, a prior-period restatement or a reclassification. Find out which before you trust equity-based ratios.

4. Receivables grow faster than revenue

Compare the growth in trade receivables with revenue growth, and track DSO (average receivables ÷ revenue × 365). Receivables outpacing sales can mean slower-paying customers, weak collection, or revenue recognised too early.

5. Profit up, operating cash flow down

One year can be timing. Two years in a row is a pattern. Check cash conversion (operating cash flow ÷ net income). If it stays well below 1.0×, find out where the cash is going: receivables, inventory, or non-cash income.

6. ROE rises while margins fall

If profitability is falling but ROE is rising, the lift is coming from leverage: the equity multiplier went up. DuPont analysis shows this in one line.

7. Negative equity reported as a ratio

With negative equity, debt-to-equity and ROE stop meaning anything. A negative D/E is not "low leverage". Report these ratios as not meaningful, and look at net debt / EBITDA and interest coverage instead.

Bonus: a blank cell is not zero. If inventory is left blank, a spreadsheet treats it as 0. Inventory days then look perfect and the quick ratio equals the current ratio, and both are wrong with no warning. A good analysis file shows "Missing input" instead of a number.
